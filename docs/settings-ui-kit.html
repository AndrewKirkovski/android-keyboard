Radio group and picker

The dropdown stops being an outlined box. An outline says “input”, and this is not one — it is a choice among a fixed list, and One UI states that as a row that opens a sheet. The box was also the only element on the screen with a border, so it read as a foreign object dropped into a card, and it wrapped to two lines because “Immediate space after suggestions & punctuation” is 46 characters and no box that width will hold it.

The current value becomes the subtitle. That is the standard two‑line row at 72 dp, so the picker introduces no new height and no new left edge, and the value gets a full line to itself instead of competing with the label for one. It takes the accent, because it is the part that changes.

The options move into a sheet, where a long option has room to be read in full rather than truncated to fit a control. The sheet is radio rows from this same kit, so nothing new is invented for it.

The radio sits in the leading slot at 22 dp, sharing the row’s text edge with an icon. Today it sits in the 48 dp icon column, so every radio option is indented 80 dp.

Tips take the card’s 12 dp inset and a 16 dp radius, so they read as a sibling of the groups rather than a foreign object. The warning variant is amber, not red — red is for destruction, and these are cautions.

What a language actually needs

The data says five things: a name, three resource slots (LanguageOptions — dictionary, transformer, voice input, each a nullable filename), a list of layouts, a multilingual flag, and deletion. That is the whole surface. Everything below follows from it.

Layouts come first. They are what somebody opens this screen to change; the three model slots are plumbing that is correct by default. Today the order is reversed.

A slot has three states, not two. A named file, a built‑in fallback, or nothing at all. Today all three render as a filename or the word “None”, so “none, and that is fine” looks identical to “none, and prediction is off.” The chip carries the difference, and only the state that needs attention takes colour. The app already computes this distinction — ConfirmResourceActionDialog checks hasBuiltInFallback — but only after you tap.

With one language, three controls are inert. The layout cannot be removed (there would be none), the language cannot be removed (there would be none), and multilingual typing needs a second language before it means anything. The card shows that, instead of offering controls that quietly do nothing: the multilingual row is disabled and says why, and the two removals are absent rather than greyed.

Destruction moves to the overflow. A full‑width filled red button is the loudest thing on the screen, and it is the action wanted least often.

Action cards and the window bar

The outer margin is smaller than the gutter. Measured: cards sit 45 px apart with 34 px to the right screen edge. An inner gap wider than the outer one makes a grid read as clipped rather than inset. The kit puts 16 dp outside and 8 dp between, so the margin always wins.

The grid is not centred. 120 px on the left against 34 px on the right, an 86 px difference that needs no ruler to see. It comes from a symmetric padding(8.dp) applied inside a window that is already inset on one side in one‑handed mode.

The tiles are enormous. 121 × 87 dp to carry a 24 dp icon and one word, so six actions fill a panel that holds twelve at 76 × 72 dp. A fixed 4‑column grid also gives a round column pitch; GridCells.Adaptive(98.dp) distributes the remainder, so the pitch lands on no particular number and lines up with nothing else on screen.

Labels are set at 100% leading. Typography.Small.copy(lineHeight = 12.sp) at a 12 sp size, so “Clipboard Manager” sets its two lines touching. Leading goes to 1.25, and the labels get shorter — the panel is already titled “All actions”, so “Manager” and “Info” add nothing.

The edit button was a 131 dp outlined pill — measured 345 px wide, a third of the bar, carrying the same visual weight as the title beside it. An outline promises a primary action. It becomes a text button, which is what a secondary action in a title bar looks like.

One thing that turned out fine: the icons do not drift when a label wraps to two lines. Measured centres across a row of one‑ and two‑line tiles were 568, 568 and 570 px. The wrap‑content Column keeps them put.

The control that moves the keyboard left and right

It is the only control on the keyboard with no container. Its neighbours all sit in filled circles — the action‑bar overflow measures 89 px across, the expand and microphone buttons about 100 — while this one is a bare 22 × 40 px stroke laid straight on the keyboard ground. It reads as decoration rather than a button.

It does not hold still. Across three captures of the same session its centre sits at y 180, y 204 and y 176 — it moves 28 px between screens, because it is pinned to the top of a full‑height column whose top depends on whether the action bar is expanded (absolutePadding(top = if (isActionsExpanded) ActionBarHeight else 0.dp)). The kit centres it on the letter rows, so it is the same distance from the thumb every time.

Nothing shares a left edge. On the actions panel the chevron’s stroke starts at x 57, the back arrow’s at 142, and the card grid at 120 — three different left edges in one view. The kit gives the gutter a single centreline and hangs the control on it.

In an action window it stays. This section previously said the opposite — that a panel’s own back arrow and this chevron are two left‑pointing glyphs in one view, so the control should disappear while a panel is open. It was built that way and then reversed after use: a control that vanishes when a panel opens reads as the panel having broken it, and one‑handed reach is exactly as awkward on a panel as it is on the keys. The two glyphs sit on different grounds — the back arrow on the panel, this on the keyboard’s gutter — which is what separates them.

It is narrower than its gutter. 40 dp against a gutter of about 51, which leaves roughly 13 dp of empty ground between the control and the nearest key. Padding cannot buy that gap: the control is pinned to the screen edge and the keys start where the one‑handed layout puts them, so padding grows the node without moving either edge. The touch target keeps its full 48 dp height.

Compose: UixManager.OneHandedOptions. The Column and its Spacer(weight) become one Box aligned CenterStart / CenterEnd; the 48 dp hit target is kept and a Surface at keyboardContainer supplies the 40 dp circle. Long‑press‑to‑exit and HideOneHandedExitButtonSetting are unchanged.

A panel of keys is made of keys

The text editor drew from Material roles, not from the keyboard. Its arrows were primary, its clipboard keys primaryContainer, its modifiers secondaryContainer — three role pairs, none of which is a colour the keyboard puts on a key. Under UixThemeWrapper those are keyboard‑scheme colours, so the file passes any check that greps for the scheme, and the panel still looks like a different product from the keys underneath it. That is the trap: compiling against the keyboard’s palette is not the same as using it.

The keyboard has exactly three key treatments, and BasicThemeProvider names them: an ordinary key is keyboardContainer, a functional key is keyboardContainerVariant, and the one accent key — enter — is primary. Glyphs are onKeyboardContainer. A panel made of keys uses those and nothing else: cursor keys are ordinary keys, clipboard and modifier keys are functional keys, and the pair the keyboard puts on a latched key — secondary, from KeyVisualStyle.StickyOn, not the enter key’s primary — is spent only on a modifier that is latched.

Key borders off is the case that catches a hardcoded colour. The same provider drops both fills to transparent and colours glyphs onBackground when a theme has borders off. A panel that reaches for keyboardContainer directly then draws filled boxes over a keyboard that draws none — the right‑hand mock above. Reading LocalThemeProvider.current.keyBorders keeps the two in step, and it is why every keyboard surface is captured on two deliberately unlike themes rather than two shades of one.

Compose: panelKeyColors() in actions/TextEditAction.kt returns the five colours as one object, and ActionKey, TogglableKey, ArrowKeys and SideKeys take them from it. LocalInspectionMode guards the provider read, because a preview has no provider and reading it throws.

The emoji page, and one selected‑item treatment across the panels

The emoji grid sat on a slab no other panel draws. Its ground was keyboardContainer — the key colour — so every emoji sat on one enormous key, and the slab’s rounded bottom corners cut the part‑row on a curve, so scrolling read as damage. Neither the actions grid nor the clipboard paints a ground at all: both sit on the one the window draws, keyboardSurfaceDim above the keyboard and keyboardSurface expanded. Containers go around items, not around pages. The slab is gone, and the grid’s content colour moves to onSurface with it — on High Contrast Yellow, onKeyboardContainer and keyboardSurface are both black, so leaving it would have been black on black.

Four left edges down one page — the slab at 0, the grid inside it at 8, the category row at 2, the search field pinned at 128 — are now one, on the Spacing scale. The search field was a fixed 128 dp pill in the window bar, dressed as a text input while being a button that switched modes. It is the panel’s width now, and takes a height floor rather than a fixed height — 48 dp, which is what the editor underneath pins itself to and what a tap target wants, so the two states agree without cutting the text off at a larger font scale.

Five selected‑item treatments existed across these surfaces. A filled secondary / onSecondary pair wins, because it is what the keyboard puts on a latched key (KeyVisualStyle.StickyOn) and because it is a defined foreground‑on‑background pair. A bare accent tint fails on DefaultLight, where primary and onSurface are two near‑blacks, and on High Contrast Yellow, where they are both white. The emoji category row keeps its pill and changes its ink, and the modes panel takes the same pill.

One exception, and it is not an oversight. A theme swatch keeps its 4 dp inversePrimary border. Every other selected item is marked by tinting it, and a swatch is a block of colour — tinting it would misreport the theme it is offering. A border is the only marker that does not lie about its own content, so the rule is: tint what is a control, outline what is a sample.

Compose: actions/EmojiAction.kt — EmojiCategoriesContainer for the pill, the panel Column for the ground, EmojiSearchBar for the field.

Redesigned

Every panel says which panel it is

Two panels had no title, for one reason. ActionWindowBar draws every panel’s back arrow and name, and it was rendered only while the keyboard was hidden. The theme switcher and keyboard modes both keep the keyboard visible, so neither ever composed one: keyboard modes showed a back arrow and a Resize keyboard button with nothing between them, and the theme switcher had no bar at all — only a floating × in a circle sitting over the keyboard’s top‑left key. Keyboard modes was given its name first, in its own header row; the flag came afterwards, with the theme panel.

Keyboard modes keeps that header, and this is the argument for it. The shared bar always draws a back arrow, and the resize tutorial needs that one arrow gone so the only way forward is the button beside it. A flag cannot express that without growing a second flag. What the panel does not get to keep is a different header: same back glyph, same title style, same height as the shared bar, so the two read as one thing even though they are built twice.

The flag lives on the window, not in the panel. ActionWindow.showTitleBarAboveKeyboard defaults to false, so every other panel composes exactly as before; the theme switcher sets it, drops its floating close button, and the bar’s own back arrow takes that job. The bar composes outside the fixed‑height content box, so the window grows upward by ActionBarHeight rather than the picker losing 40 dp — which matters, because that picker had just been cut down to fit.

Compose: Action.kt for the flag, UixManager.kt for the render condition, actions/ThemeAction.kt for the two overrides.

What this kit does not answer

Which screen each setting lives on — that is the arrangement document, already written, and it is what step 5 lays out.

The rest of the in-keyboard panels. The actions grid, its window bar, the one‑handed control, the emoji page, the text editor, keyboard modes and the clipboard’s notice and empty states are designed above; what is left untouched is the clipboard’s clip rows, the theme picker and the bug viewer, which still share Components.kt and inherit the row family unchanged. All of them need checking against a photo background, since they render over someone else’s app.

Tablet and landscape. The capture was phone portrait only.

Three exceptions to the one‑treatment‑per‑job rule, each with its reason. They are listed rather than left for a reader to find and take for oversights.

The emoji grid’s own headings — “Recent”, “Smileys & emotion” — are a 16 sp medium TextView in emoji_category.xml, not the app’s 13 sp accent section header. They are rows of a RecyclerView, drawn by the View layer because the grid scrolls thousands of glyphs, and the section header is sized for a settings page: 13 sp with a 24 dp gap above it, against grid rows of 42 dp. Matching the type here would shrink a divider that has to be findable while flicking, to buy consistency with a screen the reader is not looking at.

Two more text fields exist and neither is a panel: the font typer (FontTyperAction, a bordered box on surface) is a developer action, and the floating pre‑edit (FloatingPreEditView) is an overlay over the text being composed rather than a surface in a panel. The two that are panel fields — the clipboard’s search and the emoji page’s — share one look: a keyboardContainer pill with no border.

The unselected half of the selection treatment is not uniform, on purpose. The modes panel’s unselected labels are at full strength, because a label is small text at any size the auto‑sizer picks, so it wants 4.5:1, and 60% put it under that on seven light presets. The emoji category row’s unselected glyphs stay at 60%, because a 24 dp glyph wants 3:1 and clears it comfortably. Same rule, two thresholds, because one is text and the other is not.
