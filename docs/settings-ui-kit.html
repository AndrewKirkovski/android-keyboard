Spacing

One scale, six steps and seven names

A 4 dp base: xs 4, s 8, m 12, l 16, xl 24, xxl 32, with seven named tokens for the places a name reads better than a step — cardInset, cardGap, rowInset, rowHeight, rowHeightTwoLine, iconSlot, iconGap. A gap in a component on this scale can no longer be 9 or 19 or 21 dp because someone typed it. The scale governs what it was applied to, not the tree: the live DropDownPicker still spaces itself by 9, and the payment screen still insets by 19. Radii are not on this scale and were never meant to be: a 22 dp card and a 26 dp dialog are shapes, not spacing.

What a language actually needs

The data says five things: a name, three resource slots (LanguageOptions — dictionary, transformer, voice input, each a nullable filename), a list of layouts, a multilingual flag, and deletion. That is the whole surface. Everything below follows from it.

Layouts come first. They are what somebody opens this screen to change; the three model slots are plumbing that is correct by default. Today the order is reversed.

A slot has three states, not two. A named file, a built‑in fallback, or nothing at all. All three used to render as a name or the word “None”, so “none, and that is fine” looks identical to “none, and prediction is off.” The ask was for the chip to carry the difference, with only the state that needs attention taking colour. It is built for the two slots that have a built-in to fall back to: a dictionary with one reads “Built-in Dictionary” on the neutral chip, a voice model with one names its fallback, and only a genuine gap takes the amber MissingChip. Only the transformer slot conflates them, and ConfirmResourceActionDialog treats that kind as always having a fallback. What is not built is the type: the state is inferred from whether a string came back rather than carried, so a reader of LanguageOptions cannot tell the two apart without knowing how each slot is filled.

With one language, three controls are inert. The layout cannot be removed (there would be none), the language cannot be removed (there would be none), and multilingual typing needs a second language before it means anything. The card shows that, instead of offering controls that quietly do nothing: the multilingual row is disabled and says why, and the two removals are absent rather than greyed.

Destruction moves to the overflow. A full‑width filled red button is the loudest thing on the screen, and it is the action wanted least often.

Action cards and the window bar

The outer margin was smaller than the gutter. Measured: cards sit 45 px apart with 34 px to the right screen edge. An inner gap wider than the outer one makes a grid read as clipped rather than inset. The kit asks for 16 dp outside and 8 dp between, so the margin wins. That was tried too, and it costs each tile 4 dp of width: at 76 dp one‑handed, “Paste from clipboard” and “Voice input” take a third line. Three lines fit at the built leading, so the cost is a worse-looking panel rather than a broken one, and it is worth taking once someone can look at the result. It is 8 dp all round on the panel until the tile can afford the margin, and 8 dp at the sides with none top or bottom in the editor, which scrolls.

The grid is not centred. 120 px on the left against 34 px on the right, an 86 px difference that needs no ruler to see. It comes from a symmetric padding(8.dp) applied inside a window that is already inset on one side in one‑handed mode.

The tiles are enormous. 121 × 92 dp to carry a 24 dp icon and one word, so six actions fill a panel that holds twelve at the 84 dp it was built at — the first draft of this section said 72, and a two‑line label at the default size needs the other twelve. A fixed 4‑column grid also gives a round column pitch; GridCells.Adaptive(98.dp) distributes the remainder, so the pitch lands on no particular number and lines up with nothing else on screen.

Labels are set tighter than solid, and stay that way. Typography.Small is 14 sp and the tile overrides its leading to 12, so “Clipboard manager” sets its two lines close enough to touch. This section asked for 1.25 and it was tried; it does not fit the tile it is for. A 24 dp icon in an 84 dp tile leaves the label 52 dp, and three lines at 1.25 need 52.5 — so at every font scale, including the default, a three‑line label is cut, and vertical overflow cuts rather than ellipsising. The theme switcher’s 32 dp glyph leaves 44. Raising the leading needs a taller tile or shorter labels first, and the second half of that was the other thing this paragraph asked for and never got: the panel is titled “All actions”, so “manager” and “info” still add nothing.

The edit button was a 131 dp outlined pill — measured 345 px wide, a third of the bar, carrying the same visual weight as the title beside it. An outline promises a primary action. It becomes a text button, which is what a secondary action in a title bar looks like.

One thing that turned out fine: the icons do not drift when a label wraps to two lines. Measured centres across a row of one‑ and two‑line tiles were 568, 568 and 570 px. The label is centred in a column that fills the tile, so the icon above it stays on the tile’s centreline whatever the label does.

A panel of keys is made of keys

The text editor drew from Material roles, not from the keyboard. Its arrows were primary, its clipboard keys primaryContainer, its modifiers secondaryContainer — three role pairs, none of which is a colour the keyboard puts on a key. Under UixThemeWrapper those are keyboard‑scheme colours, so the file passes any check that greps for the scheme, and the panel still looks like a different product from the keys underneath it. That is the trap: compiling against the keyboard’s palette is not the same as using it.

A panel of keys needs three of the keyboard’s treatments, and a fourth for a latched one. KeyVisualStyle has eight values and BasicThemeProvider gives every one of them a style; three carry the panel. An ordinary key is keyboardContainer, a functional key is keyboardContainerVariant, and the one accent key — enter — is primary. Expert mode quietens it rather than flattening it: enter drops to outline at 10%, and its glyph — an icon, so tinted by enterKeyForeground rather than by the descriptor’s label colour — to onBackground at 33%, where a functional key’s glyph goes to 20% and a letter’s goes transparent while its fill stays. Enter stays the most legible thing on the keyboard. Glyphs are onKeyboardContainer. A panel made of keys uses those and nothing else: cursor keys are ordinary keys, clipboard and modifier keys are functional keys, and the pair the keyboard puts on a latched key — secondary, from KeyVisualStyle.StickyOn, not the enter key’s primary — is spent only on a modifier that is latched.

Key borders off is the case that catches a hardcoded colour. The same provider drops both fills to transparent and colours glyphs onBackground when a theme has borders off. A panel that reaches for keyboardContainer directly then draws filled boxes over a keyboard that draws none — the right‑hand mock above. Reading LocalThemeProvider.current.keyBorders keeps the two in step, and it is why every keyboard surface is captured on two deliberately unlike themes rather than two shades of one.

Compose: panelKeyColors() in actions/TextEditAction.kt returns the five colours as one object, and ActionKey, TogglableKey, ArrowKeys and SideKeys take them from it. LocalInspectionMode guards the provider read, because a preview has no provider and reading it throws.

The emoji page, and one selected‑item treatment across the panels

The emoji grid sat on a slab no other panel draws. Its ground was keyboardContainer — the key colour — so every emoji sat on one enormous key, and the slab’s rounded bottom corners cut the part‑row on a curve, so scrolling read as damage. Neither the actions grid nor the clipboard paints a ground at all: both sit on the one the window draws, keyboardSurfaceDim above the keyboard and keyboardSurface expanded. Containers go around items, not around pages. The slab is gone, and the grid’s content colour moves to onSurface with it — on High Contrast Yellow, onKeyboardContainer and keyboardSurface are both black, so leaving it would have been black on black.

Four left edges down one page — the slab at 0, the grid inside it at 8, the category row at 2, the search field pinned at 128 — are now one, on the Spacing scale — except the category row, whose own Spacing.s sits inside the navigation row’s, so its first item’s box starts at 16 — the 24 dp glyph is centred in an IconButton inside that, so its ink starts nearer 28, and at 76 when the letters key, which is exactly 48 dp and sits flush against the row, precedes it. The search field was a fixed 128 dp pill in the window bar, dressed as a text input while being a button that switched modes. It is the panel’s width now, and takes a height floor rather than a fixed height — 48 dp, which is what the editor underneath pins itself to and what a tap target wants, so the two states agree without cutting the text off at a larger font scale.

Five selected‑item treatments existed across these surfaces. A filled secondary / onSecondary pair wins, because it is what the keyboard puts on a latched key (KeyVisualStyle.StickyOn) and because it is a defined foreground‑on‑background pair. A bare accent tint fails on DefaultLightScheme — “Light Mode” — where primary and onSurface are two near‑blacks, and on High Contrast Yellow, where they are both white. The emoji category row keeps its fully round pill and changes its ink; the modes panel takes the same pair on a marker that fills its tile — the full width by 48 of the tile’s 54 dp — at a 12 dp corner radius. The colour is shared and the shape is not.

One exception, and it is not an oversight. A theme swatch keeps its 4 dp border, drawn in primary: inversePrimary was the first choice, and the settings app’s palette does not define that role — nor did the nineteen static presets, until the builders in ColorScheme.kt began filling it — so the border marking the theme in use came out Material’s baseline purple on both surfaces it is drawn on. Every other selected item is marked by tinting it, and a swatch is a block of colour — tinting it would misreport the theme it is offering. A border is the only marker that does not lie about its own content, so the rule is: tint what is a control, outline what is a sample.

Every panel says which panel it is

Three panels had no title, for one reason. ActionWindowBar draws every panel’s back arrow and name, and it was rendered only while the keyboard was hidden. The theme switcher, keyboard modes and Debug info all keep the keyboard visible, so none ever composed one, and each was answered separately. Keyboard modes was first: it showed a back arrow and a Resize keyboard button with nothing between them until it was given its name in a header row of its own. The theme switcher had it worse and was answered later, with the flag — no bar at all, only a floating × in a circle sitting over the keyboard’s top‑left key. Debug info was the one nobody looked at, because it is a developer surface: it declared a windowName() and drew it nowhere. It takes the same flag.

Keyboard modes keeps that header, and this is the argument for it. The shared bar always draws a back arrow, and the resize tutorial needs that one arrow gone so the only way forward is the button beside it. A flag cannot express that without growing a second flag. What the panel does not get to keep is a different header: same back glyph, same title style, same height as the shared bar, so the two read as one thing even though they are built twice.

The flag lives on the window, not in the panel. ActionWindow.showTitleBarAboveKeyboard defaults to false, so every panel that does not set it composes exactly as before. Two set it — the theme switcher and Debug info — and both drop their floating close button, because the bar’s own back arrow takes that job. The bar composes outside the fixed‑height content box, so the window grows upward by ActionBarHeight rather than the picker losing 40 dp — which matters, because that picker had just been cut down to fit.

Compose: Action.kt for the flag, UixManager.kt for the render condition, and actions/ThemeAction.kt and actions/MemDebugAction.kt for the two overrides apiece. actions/KeyboardSizingActions.kt is the third panel the bar would skip, and it keeps its own header instead.

Redesigned

The clipboard, and what a panel says when it holds nothing

Its notice was five lines of product sentences. Two of the three said what the heading above them and the button below them already said — how to switch the feature on, and that it can be configured elsewhere, from a panel with no route to elsewhere. What is left is the part a reader cannot get from the two controls around it: what clipboard history does, and what it keeps. The card is centred rather than pinned to the top, because this panel ends where the keyboard begins and a lone card at the top leaves the bottom half of it visibly blank.

It has two empty states and drew one of them. A search with no matches said so, in italic — the last italic text on any settings surface. With history on and nothing copied the panel was a blank rectangle, and what stood in for an empty state there was a pinned clip reading “Clipboard entries will appear here”: a hardcoded English string sitting in the history as though the reader had copied it, which could be pinned, pasted and deleted like a real one, and which only ever appeared before the first save — so a history that had clips and no longer does still drew nothing. An empty state is not a list item.

Four of them across three panels, one treatment and one placement. The clipboard’s two, the emoji page’s search with no matches — which arrived as a section heading, because it was pushed into the grid as a category — and the actions grid with no actions, which was announced by the accent section header. All four are the app’s empty state now, centred in a panel that ends where the keyboard begins.

And the control that clears it is a bin. It wore the close glyph, two icons from the back arrow that dismisses the panel, on a button whose three outcomes are clear the clips, unpin all of them, or switch the feature off. The emoji page’s clear‑recents button had the same fault and the same fix.

One thing deliberately left: the notice card keeps its accent fill. An empty list should not be accent‑coloured — that is the rule the settings empty state was written for — but this is not an empty list. It is a feature that is off, with the button that turns it on inside the card, which is what the accent is for.

Compose: actions/clipboard/ClipboardHistoryAction.kt — ClipboardNotice for the three one‑card states, ClipboardEmptyState for the empty ones, and isTheOldEmptyStateEntry(), which drops the pinned clip from histories that already saved it and from a settings backup that restores one.

What this kit does not answer

Which screen each setting lives on — that is the arrangement document, already written, and it is what step 5 lays out.

What is designed above: the actions grid and its window bar, the one‑handed control, the emoji page, the text editor, keyboard modes, the theme panel’s title bar and headings, and the clipboard’s notice and empty states.

What is not: three sets of items inside those panels. A clip row (ClipboardEntryView) is a hand‑built surface with its own pin and remove controls. A theme swatch (ThemePreview) is a box with a border and a rendering of the theme inside it. The bug viewer is monospace text and text buttons, and keeps its own debug‑styled empty state, being a developer surface. None of the three is a settings row, so none of them inherits the row family; they were left because nothing in this pass needed them, not because they were checked and passed.

All of these render over someone else’s app, and none has been checked against a photo background.

Tablet and landscape. The capture was phone portrait only.

Three exceptions to the one‑treatment‑per‑job rule, each with its reason. They are listed rather than left for a reader to find and take for oversights.

The emoji grid’s own headings — “Recent”, “Smileys & emotion” — are a 16 sp medium TextView in emoji_category.xml, not the app’s 13 sp accent section header. They are rows of a RecyclerView, drawn by the View layer because the grid scrolls thousands of glyphs, and the section header is sized for a settings page: 13 sp with a 24 dp gap above it, against grid rows of 42 dp. Matching the type here would shrink a divider that has to be findable while flicking, to buy consistency with a screen the reader is not looking at.

Two more text fields exist. The floating pre‑edit (FloatingPreEditView) is an overlay over the text being composed rather than a surface in a panel. The font typer (FontTyperAction, a bordered box on surface) is a panel, and one this pass did not audit: it is not in the disabled set, so it ships in More actions as “WordStyles”, and its field is the one place a keyboard text field still wears a border. The two that are panel fields — the clipboard’s search and the emoji page’s — share one look: a keyboardContainer pill with no border. They keep that fill on a theme with key borders off, and so do the action tiles and the mic and expand buttons: the rule that empties a key on those themes is a rule about keys. A control keeps its container, which is what tells it apart from the keys it sits above.

The unselected half of the selection treatment is not uniform, on purpose. The modes panel’s unselected labels are at full strength, because a label is small text at any size the auto‑sizer picks, so it wants 4.5:1, and 60% put it under that on seven light presets. The emoji category row’s unselected glyphs went the same way, and for the same reason: a 24 dp glyph is held to 3:1 rather than 4.5, and the 60% of onBackground that used to tint it measured 3.7 to 4.5 across the light presets — a clear pass. What settles it is that the filled pill already says which one is selected, so the alpha said it twice. Both are full strength, and in both the filled marker is what says which one is selected — a round pill on the emoji row, a tile-filling rectangle at a 12 dp radius on the modes panel.
